## 1. Introduction

Autofocus (AF) is a process through which the camera can determine automatically a lens position to focus on the region of interest (RoI). It is a crucial step in image capturing, since once an image is already captured in a defocused and blurry state, it is then difficult to restore its sharpness

through any post-processing due to the varying effects of blur influenced by factors such as camera aperture and radial distortion \[ 1 \]. In addition, the beauty of a moment often lies in its fleeting nature, which thus imposes an imperative demand for the fast focusing speed of an AF algorithm. However, due to the instability in focusing systems incurred by some interference factors, the AF mechanism of the camera lens may fail to accurately lock onto the focal point, resulting in a repeated movement of the lens within a certain range. This phenomenon, as illustrated in Fig. 1, is referred to as * * *focus* * * *hunting* * * *(FH)*, whereby the camera is unable to identify the focus plane and thus drives the lens forward and backward through a set range of focus hunting for it. It then causes variations in the image sharpness and changes the lens's field of view (FoV), ultimately leading to an unstable focusing experience with longer focusing time, which is a common issue in the traditional AF-assisted cameras \[ 2 \]. It is thus of great importance to develop an AF algorithm that not only achieves a higher in-focus accuracy but can also stabilize the trajectory of lens movement with a faster convergence speed to the in-focus position, thus reducing the probability of occurrence of focus hunting \[ 43, 48 \].

There is a large body of work on the traditional autofocus methods, generally falling into two categories: searchbased and phase-based methods. Search-based AF methods \[ 40, 42 \] search within a range of focus distances to locate the lens position of the extreme sharpness value, which is then determined as the in-focus lens position. Thus, they need to move the lens back and forth, which leads to a highly accurate identification of the in-focus position, but with an annoyingly slow focusing procedure and inevitably the occurrence of focus hunting. Phase-based approaches, on the other hand, rely on the dual-pixel (DP) sensors to obtain the phase data from the left and right sub-images, which is then leveraged to predict the lens movement and determine the in-focus position in a single step of lens

Though accelerating the focusing procedure, the phase data obtained by sensors is highly susceptible to noise, leading to a relatively low in-focus accuracy. Especially in some extreme cases, e.g., the low-light conditions, the phase detection may need to be executed multiple times at different lens positions to predict a more accurate in-focus position, still at the risk of focus hunting. Recent attempts \[ 7, 16 \] have concentrated on addressing the above trade-off between accuracy and speed, by exploiting the expressive power of neural networks to directly learn a mapping from the captured image at any lens position to the predicted in-focus position. However, when we directly treat the learned neural network as a lens movement policy and apply it at multiple steps to adjust the lens movement, we notice that some of the resulting trajectories still suffer from the back-and-forth movement of the lens, as shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2, due to the lack of leveraging the sequential information obtained from multiple exploration steps and the ignorance of considering future effects of the current prediction. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL), with successful applications in sequential decision-making tasks such as autonomous driving \[ 12 \] and robot control \[ 8 \], has enabled the agent to adaptively adjust its policy through iterative interactions with the environment, based both on the immediate reward of current decision step and the long-term reward over the entire sequence of decisions.

Inspired by this, we propose in this paper an expert regularized DRL-based approach for camera autofocus, which is able to utilize the sequential information of lens movement trajectory to both enhance the multi-step in-focus prediction accuracy and reduce the chance of focus hunting. Our proposed method generally follows an actor-critic framework, within which the actor network (i.e., policy network) takes the captured left and right dual-pixel images with the positional information of RoI and lens as the state input, and sequentially generates a trajectory by making decisions on the lens movement. The critic network, on the other hand, receives the same input as the actor network and estimates the future expected accumulated reward given the current state, which is used to evaluate the action predicted by the actor network and thus eliminate suboptimal actions. To accelerate the DRL's training with a higher sample efficiency, we initialize the actor network with a pre-trained single-step prediction network based on the MobileNet-V2 architecture \[ 33 \], where to be more consistent with the environment transition in AF tasks, the output of absolute in-focus position distribution as used in \[ 7 \] is modified to the relative lens movement distribution. The critic network also takes the same MobileNet-V2 architecture. To further stabilize the DRL's training with lower occurrence of focus hunting in the resulting lens movement trajectory, we generate some offline trajectories based on prior knowledge to avoid focus hunting, which are then leveraged as an offline dataset of expert trajectories to regularize the actor network's training. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: - To overcome the limitation of single-step prediction networks, we formulate AF as a multi-step task and then propose a DRL-based framework to fully utilize the sequential information of lens movement trajectory and adjust the policy based on the immediate and long-term rewards. - We initialize the policy with a pre-trained single-step pre-

diction network, where the network is improved by modifying the output of absolute in-focus position distribution to relative lens movement distribution to establish a better mapping between input images and lens movement. - We construct an offline dataset of expert trajectories based on prior knowledge, regularizing the training of the policy network towards reducing occurrence of focus hunting. - Empirical evaluations demonstrate that our method outperforms state-of-the-art AF methods on public benchmarks, with higher single- and multi-step prediction accuracy, and a significant reduction of focus hunting rate.

## 2. Related Works

Traditional passive AF methods can generally be categorized into search-based and phase-based approaches. Search-based approaches calculate and maximize the sharpness of the images based on image statistics \[ 9, 21, 40, 42, 53 \] or frequency-domain image representations \[ 18, 24, 25, 47, 50 \] to determine the optimal lens position. While showing high accuracy, these approaches are time-consuming. Additionally, search-based methods inevitably lead to focus hunting, since the peak must be surpassed to locate the exact focus point. Though some methods \[ 14, 39 \] can reduce the search steps, they cannot solve the problem of focus hunting completely, or can only work on some specific devices. With the development of dual-pixel sensors, phase-based methods have become the primary AF method in modern digital cameras \[ 37 \]. With the phase information of the two (left/right) split pixels, the disparity can be calculated to determine the lens movement. Consequently, phase-based approaches can move the lens to the in-focus lens position with a single image capture, which is more efficient than search-based approaches. However, factors that are difficult to model, such as lens shading, can affect the calculation of phase, leading to lower accuracy. Numerous works adopt learning-based methods to address the trade-off between accuracy and speed. But these works either apply to specific tasks like microscopy images \[ 31, 51 \], or are based on phase information obtained by specific hardware \[ 17, 46 \], which are difficult to reproduce and compare. The above methods concentrate on the processed sharpness metrics or phase data, not sufficiently using the information from pictures. Inspired by the stereo vision \[ 19, 20 \], information obtained from dual-pixel pictures has found increasingly widespread applications in the computer vision community, such as monocular depth estimation \[ 11, 22 \] and defocus deblurring \[ 1 \]. In AF, Herrmann * * *et* * * *al*. \[ 16 \] utilize convolutional networks to predict the in-focus position from input images and provide a large-scale AF dataset. Choi * et al*. \[ 7 \] improve Herrmann's network by incorporating the region of interest position encoding (RoI-PE) and the lens position encoding (Lens-PE), significantly reducing the size while achieving state-of-the-art performance.

Both methods, similar to the phase-based approaches, essentially employ the DP data to estimate the disparity and predict the lens position. But the difference is that phasebased approaches estimate the lens movement through disparity rather than directly predicting the next lens position. Meanwhile, though \[ 7, 16 \] discuss the multi-step prediction, they only address the accuracy of multi-step prediction without considering the potential for focus hunting, which can greatly affect the speed and accuracy of the AF model. The main reason for focus hunting in learning-based methods is the lack of consideration of the sequential information and the future effects of the actions. Yan * et al*. \[ 49 \] and Wang * * *et* * * *al*. \[ 46 \] adopt the long short-term memory (LSTM) to enhance the model's ability to incorporate previous information. With extra computation, the ability of LSTM to capture the 2-dimension data is still limited. The paradigm of predicting actions from states and the need to incorporate sequential information and future effects inspire us to use reinforcement learning (RL) in AF. Though Yu * et* *al*. \[ 54 \] and Chan * et al*. \[ 6 \] have proposed DRL-based autofocus methods, they both concentrate on the design of reward functions and do not fully leverage the advantages of RL. Wang * et al*. \[ 46 \] adopt RL in their work to dynamically choose the RoI, rather than predicting the lens position or movement. To the best of our knowledge, little research has been conducted to reduce the occurrence of focus hunting while accelerating the multi-step prediction with a higher accuracy for AF tasks.

## 4. Experiments

In the experiments, our actor and critic networks are built upon MobileNetV2 \[ 33 \]. We reproduce the models for \[ 16 \] and \[ 7 \], since no open-source code is available. In the multistep prediction setting, we only consider the model obtained by \[ 7 \], since the multi-step model given by \[ 16 \] must be retrained for each step, which is impractical. More results and analysis are provided in Sec. 9 in our supplementary. **Dataset.** We adopt the publicly available AF dataset constructed by Herrmann * * *et* * * *al*. \[ 16 \]. This dataset comprises 51 scenes, each containing 10 stacks with varying compositions. All instances within the dataset are captured at * * *n* = 49 focal depths, uniformly sampled in the inverse depth space, ranging from 0.102 to 3.91 meters, captured using the Google Pixel 3 smartphones. To create the training and test sets, we extract and sample 128 * ×* 128 patches with a stride of 96 from the central camera, resulting in a total of 3,341,163 patches for the training set and 382,445 patches for the test set. Patches with the same spatial position within one image and 49 different focal depths constitute a single focal stack. Please refer to \[ 16 \] for additional details about the dataset, and refer to Sec. 6 in our supplementary for the labeling method adopted in this paper. **Evaluation** ** ** **metric.** We follow the evaluation metric proposed by Scharstein * * *et* * * *al*. \[ 34 \], which is used for Middlebury stereo dataset and adopted by Herrmann * et al*. \[ 16 \] and Choi * * *et* * * *al*. \[ 7 \] to evaluate the AF model's prediction accuracy. In particular, this metric is calculated by the proportion of patches whose stop positions are within 0, 1, 2, and 4 indices away from the in-focus index, which can be considered in the depth of field (DoF). The mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) are also

used to evaluate the AF model's performance. To evaluate the focus hunting (FH), we retain every focusing trajectory and calculate the direction and distance of each lens movement. If the direction of a current movement is opposite to that of the previous one in the trajectory, we consider an occurrence of FH in that trajectory. Since the actor network obtained by our DRL and the model in \[ 7 \] share the same architecture and similar input/output, we can simply regard the number of steps to reach the in-focus position as the focus speed. We calculate the images captured at all initial positions of the lens to account for accuracy and FH rate. **Implementation** ** ** **detail.** We use PyTorch \[ 29 \] and TorchRL \[ 4 \] for our implementation. For deep learning models, we use the Adam optimizer \[ 23 \] with a learning rate $$ of 10−3, (\beta1, \beta2) = (0.5, 0.999), a batch size of 128, and $$ cosine learning rate decay \[ 26 \] for 10k iterations. For reinforcement learning models, we adopt the PPO-CLIP \[ 35 \] algorithm. The initial learning rate is set to 1 *×* 10 *−* 5, and the clip epsilon is 0.2, a batch size of 32, Adam optimizer with the same ( *β* 1 *, β* 2 ) as deep learning. The maximum episode length of RL is set to 4. ## 5. Conclusion

We have proposed a DRL-based autofocus model with expert trajectory regularization that can both improve the prediction accuracy and reduce the focus hunting rate, which took an actor-critic framework based on the pretrained MobileNet-V2 architecture for single-step prediction. For the actor network, we further modified the absolute position label in \[ 7, 16 \] with the relative movement label method. We also constructed an offline dataset of expert trajectories based on prior knowledge, regularizing the policy network training toward reducing focus hunting. Experimental results have clearly demonstrated the effectiveness of our proposed method, in terms of the multi-step prediction accuracy and the occurrence of focus hunting.
