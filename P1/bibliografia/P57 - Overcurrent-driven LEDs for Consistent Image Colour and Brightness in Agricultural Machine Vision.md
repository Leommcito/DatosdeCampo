# A R T I C L E I N F O

*Keywords: * Agriculture imaging Artificial illumination Camera-flash synchronization Computer vision Motion blur Variable lighting

# A B S T R A C T

Machine vision systems are being utilized extensively in agriculture applications. Daytime imaging in outdoor field conditions presents challenges such as variable lighting and colour inconsistencies due to sunlight. Motion blur can occur due to vehicle movement and vibrations from ground terrain. A camera system with active lighting can be a solution to overcome these challenges. In this study, the usage of over-current driven LEDs to produce a powerful flash was investigated as a viable light source for daytime imaging. The current drawn by an LED was increased by a factor of six times its normal rating resulting in increased illuminance. A circuit was designed for storing and releasing energy to the LEDs for a strobe-like effect and a controller was used for synchronizing the strobe with a camera to acquire images. The system was deployed in an apple orchard on three days in summer of 2020. Images were taken throughout the day in both sunny and cloudy conditions of different canopy structures. There was substantial improvement in image brightness and colour consistency by using the LED flashes. Images captured by the prototype system during an 11-hour period showed an average decrease of 85% in standard deviation for the Hue-Saturation-Value (HSV) channels compared to that of the auto-exposure setting. Additionally, the prototype system was able to fix motion blur in images averaging 7 mm in error for a stereo vision application with the camera moving at 7 km/hr. These results show that the designed LED flash system can reduce the undesirable effects of lighting variability and motion blur in images stemming from outdoor field conditions. **1.** ** ** **Introduction **

With an increase in global competition, rising food demand from population growth, and expectation of higher quality agricultural products by consumers, more farmers are turning towards technologies in precision agriculture and automation to increase output and effi­ ciency. Machine vision systems have become prominent remote sensing devices for collecting and analyzing data in agricultural applications. From grading and sorting of fruit in packing houses, to robotic har­ vesting, yield estimation, and large-scale monitoring of crops using drones, camera systems are becoming widely utilized in the agricultural sector. With the rise of artificial intelligence and machine learning in applications of computer vision, images are a prime source of data which can help farmers improve management decision-making. Obtaining high-quality images is often a crucial step for many computer vision tasks. Image quality can influence the performance of computer vision algorithms and the amount of time spent on post-

processing of data. Factors contributing to a lower image quality can be environmental, such as variable lighting, or application-specific, such as motion blur in images caused by a mobile platform. In a review of different fruit detection systems by Gongal et al. (2015), variable lighting condition was often labeled as the culprit for limiting the suc­ cess of a study, along with fruit occlusion and fruit clustering. In ap­ plications like tree fruit yield estimation, where imaging is done on multiple rows of an orchard, it is essential for each image to have a uniformly lit foreground with minimal background visibility to prevent counting of fruit in other rows. During image processing, this uniformity can establish stable colour segmentation and detection of fruits in sequential images ( Wang et al., 2013 ). With the increasing utilization of computer vision algorithms for applications in digital agriculture, it is beneficial to develop machine vision systems that can overcome lighting variability in the field. With the right aperture and shutter duration settings to block the sun ' s influence, xenon flash lamps have been used to provide more uniform illumination for daytime agricultural imaging ( Nuske et al., 2014; Stein et al., 2016 ). One drawback with xenon flash lamps is that a high enough voltage (around 300 V) is required to keep the xenon gas ionized. This means a finite amount of time is needed to charge the capacitors to a high voltage before the flashes can be triggered, and there is limited flexibility for controlling the flash duration as the voltage drops below the ionization threshold. Previous studies with LEDs have mainly focused on outdoor night­ time imaging ( Fu et al., 2015; Linker & Kelman, 2015; Payne et al., 2014 ). Daytime imaging with LEDs has usually included an external apparatus which houses the machine vision system and LEDs to block or reduce the effects of sunlight on images. Gongal et al. (2016) used a full canopy cover made with tarpaulin to image individual trees. Choi et al. (2016) used a metal shield housing to detect dropped fruit under tree canopy. Even though LEDs are less powerful than xenon lamps, they are more efficient in terms of lumen output per wattage. They also have potential for higher flash repetition rate over xenon lamps. Furthermore, LEDs are semiconductor devices, so their illumination duration and power are directly controlled by the input current and can therefore be switched on and off for a desired length of time. Artificial illumination is not the only method that can be used to overcome the challenges of variable lighting in images for computer vision tasks. Recent advances in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have made it possible to get high fruit detection accuracy without the use of artificial lighting. Even though CNNs have made significant progress in fruit detection under natural lighting, a powerful light source allows the shortening of shutter duration on a camera which has several benefits in agricultural applications. First, it reduces the negative in­ fluence of sunlight exposure like drop shadow effects and colour in­ consistencies. Sivaraman & Murthy (2018) showed that pretrained networks such as VGG16 ( Simonyan & Zisserman, 2015 ) and ResNet ( He et al., 2015 ) can lack robustness to lighting variations when trained on datasets such as ImageNet ( Deng et al., 2009 ), giving considerable instability in their predictions. An investigation by Loh & Chan (2019) on training CNNs with both bright and low-light data showed that low light altered object features when looking at the activation maps of CNNs. Second, a shorter shutter duration and active light source can help to remove background scenery as it can be undesirable in fruit counting tasks to have subsequent rows of a field visible in an image. This is especially noticeable when trees have sparse canopies, or the field has short row spacings. Third, a shorter shutter duration helps to reduce motion blur in images. Similar to lighting variation, motion blur can alter object features and have a negative impact when training neural networks. CNN models like AlexNet ( Krizhevsky et al., 2012 ), VGG-Face ( Parkhi et al., 2015 ), and GoogLeNet ( Szegedy et al., 2014 ) showed significant decrease in accuracy with increasing blur levels likely due to the removal of edges and texture ( Karahan et al., 2016 ). In outdoor agricultural applications, the source of the motion blur can be from a fast-moving camera platform or vibrations due to the field terrain. Minimizing such blur effects can be important when precise segmenta­ tion of object boundaries is needed, such as determining the boundaries of fruit for sizing. The limitation in using LEDs for daytime imaging is they typically cannot provide enough illumination, under normal operating condi­ tions, to overcome the sun ' s influence. However, LEDs can be over- current driven using a pulsing mechanism to increase their power output significantly so long as proper thermal management is in place. This method has been explored in particle imaging velocimetry (PIV) where LEDs were pulsed for durations between 1 and 250 µ s achieving drive currents of up to 150 amps ( Buchmann et al., 2012; Willert et al., 2010 ). The short pulses allowed significant time for the LED die to cool down which enabled much higher drive currents. The same concept can be applied to chip-on-board LED modules for a high-power strobe effect

in outdoor imaging. The primary goal of this study was to develop an LED strobing mechanism integrated into a machine vision system for overcoming lighting variability and motion blur in daytime field imaging. Specific objectives were to:

1. Design the flash system circuitry and control with pulsed over-  current driven LEDs for continuous field operation, 2. Conduct field experiment using the developed system to validate  image colour and brightness consistency in various outdoor illumi­ nation conditions, and 3. Analyze capability of the system in decreasing motion blur due to  velocity and vibrations from the imaging platform. **2.** ** ** **Materials and methods **

The machine vision system in Fig. 1 was developed to provide con­ stant illumination for images taken in an apple orchard. The system was designed to be used for other field applications as well. There were three main components in the system: (1) the image acquisition hardware which included the camera system and LED panel, (2) the flash circuit and power delivery system, and (3) the control system for image acquisition and strobe synchronization. *2.1.* * * *Image acquisition hardware *

To develop and test the customized flash system, a pair of industrial- grade 8.9 MP CMOS colour cameras with global shutter were used (model BFS-U3-88S6C-C, FLIR Systems, Wilsonville, OR, USA). A pair of 8 mm fixed focal length lenses (model NMV-8 M1, NAVITAR Inc., Rochester, NY, USA) were mounted onto the cameras. The cameras had internal settings which were important for image quality and synchro­ nization with the flash unit. In this study, three camera parameters were considered for adjusting image brightness: aperture, exposure time, and gain. The gain was set to a low setting of one since increasing the gain could also introduce digital noise and artifacts in the image. The aper­ ture and shutter duration were adjusted in combination with the LED flash system to remove as much of the background scene and sunlight as possible while allowing the active lighting source to sufficiently expose the foreground area. The chosen aperture was f/4 and a manual expo­ sure time set to 200 µ s with the cameras being placed in the range of 1.22 - 2.44 m away from their target. Fig. 2 shows how these camera settings and the flashes influenced images taken of an apple tree at different distances. If the cameras moved closer than 1.22 m to the target, the flashes would saturate objects in the image, such as fruit, while more than 2.44 m from the target would make the entire scene too dark. *2.2.* * * *LED flash system *

In total, 16 chip-on-board LEDs (model XLamp CMA3090, Cree Inc., Durham, NC, USA) were used for the flash system. Each LED had a maximum drive current of 3.6 amps (A) at 48 V (V) totaling 173 W (W) of power. To create a high-power strobe effect, the voltage was increased to 80 V and pulsed using a microcontroller to turn on a MOSFET switch for a total flash duration of 300 µ s. At 80 V the current draw was measured to be near 20 A which gave an output power of 1.6 kW per LED, totaling 25.6 kW for a single strobe. Based on these specifications, a custom LED driver was developed on a printed circuit board (PCB). The list of circuit components used for the LED driver is given in Table 1. The entire machine vision system ran on the Robot Operating System (ROS) platform ( Quigley et al., 2009 ) version Kinetic Kame ( ROS, 2016 ) installed under Ubuntu Linux version 16.04. ROS contained third-party packages for FLIR camera drivers ( Ros-drivers, 2018 ) which were used for this system. The drivers included the necessary configurable pa­ rameters such as hardware-enabled triggering and camera exposure duration. An Arduino Mega 2560 microcontroller board (Arduino LLC, Somerville, MA, USA) was used for synchronizing the flash system with the camera. Fig. 4 A shows a timing diagram of how the camera and flash were synchronized to capture a single image using the Arduino. At time zero the camera drivers were started with hardware-enabled trigger and shutter duration set to 200 µ s. Serial communication between the Arduino and the computer was also opened through a USB interface. For a duration of nearly 200 ms, the flash circuit charged the capacitors. At 200 ms the Arduino triggered the capacitors to discharge. After 50 µ s the Arduino triggered the cameras to acquire an image. The LEDs remained on for the next 250 µ s as the camera sensor started the integration of incoming light. Finally, the LEDs were turned off and the flash circuit started to charge the capacitors again. The extra 50 µ s flash duration before the camera trigger was needed so the LEDs had enough time to

reach full power before camera exposure started, this can be seen in Fig. 4 B where an oscilloscope reading of the LEDs turning on (top signal in blue) shows a little over 25 µ s for the LED current to fully ramp up. The flashes were also kept on 50 µ s after the camera shutter was signaled to close to ensure camera exposure ended before the LEDs turned off. The setup with 16 LEDs required a recharge time of 196 ms for a single flash, thus achieving a maximum strobe rate of 5 Hz. *2.4.* * * *Experiment setups *

Four experiments were conducted to evaluate the performance of the flash system: (1) the relationship between LED power output and image colour and brightness, (2) short-term stability of continuous strobing of flash system, (3) colour and brightness consistency in images during daytime imaging, and (4) motion blur effects in images due to vehicle speed and vibrations. Experiments (1) and (2) were done in a laboratory. Experiments (3) and (4) were conducted in an orchard at the Russell E. Larson Agricultural Research Center (Pennsylvania Furnace, PA, USA). A colour rendition chart (ColorChecker Classic model MSCCC, X-Rite Inc., Grand Rapids, MI, USA) was used to analyze the images. For the experiments using the chart, three of the colour patches were used for image analysis: blue (#13), green (#14), and red (#15). Images of the colour patches were converted to Hue-Saturation-Value (HSV) compo­ nents and variations in these channels were measured. Variability in the hue channel across images determined colour consistency and vari­ ability in the value channel determined lighting consistency.

*2.4.1.* * * *Determining relationship between LED power output and image * *colour and brightness * It was important to know whether over-current driving the LEDs would affect image colour and what the power to brightness relationship was. The camera system and one of the LEDs were used to conduct an experiment in a laboratory setting which had constant ambient fluo­ rescent lighting. An oscilloscope (model TBS-1072B, Tektronix Inc., Beaverton, OR, USA) with a voltage probe (model TPP0101) and an amp meter probe (model CC-65, Hantek Electronic Co., Qingdao, Shandong, China) recorded the voltage and current readings for each flash. The voltage probe was connected to a capacitor and the amp meter probe clamped around the input power wire to the LED. Starting at 50 V and incrementing by 5 V (for a total of 20 increments) the LED was strobed and an image was captured until there was irreversible damage to the LED. The colour rendition chart was placed at a fixed distance (0.86 m) in front of the camera system to capture changes in the image brightness and colour as the power to the LED was increased. For each strobe, the voltage, current, and average HSV values of the red, blue, and green colours of the rendition chart were recorded in a total of 20 images.

*2.4.2.* * * *Short-term stability of continuous strobing of flash system * When imaging large acreages, it is important for the continuous operation of the camera and flash system to record images with consistent quality. To ensure no short-term degradation of the LEDs during continuous operation, one flash panel containing four LEDs was strobed for five hours at a rate of 5 Hz. The experiment was conducted in a laboratory setting which had constant ambient fluorescent lighting. An image of the colour rendition chart was captured at every minute, totaling 300 images for the duration of the experiment. The average HSV values of the red, blue, and green square patches were then recorded for each image. Finally, a time-lapse comparison was made of the average HSV values.

The high-power flash system, which enabled the shortening of shutter duration on the camera, reduced motion blur in both horizontal and vertical directions in images. *3.5.* * * *Limitations and possible improvements *

The flash circuit operates at 5 Hz by using a single 80 V DC step-up converter to charge 16 capacitors. If desired, two converters can be used, each charging half of the capacitor bank, to obtain a 10 Hz strobe rate. It is also possible that a single power supply capable of delivering higher amperes can be used to charge multiple capacitors at once. Another method to improve performance is through a better PCB design. Wilson et al. (2014) noted in their study that a well-designed, compact PCB providing wide, short traces for high current paths could reduce both parasitics and ohmic losses and thereby improve their circuit performance. In this study, the flash system was used intermittently during two summer seasons and no noticeable degradation was observed. Docu­ mentation from the LED manufacturer ( Cree Inc., 2018 ) on over-current driving suggests that for repetitive pulsing, electromigration in the metal contact interconnects, caused by the high current densities, will over time contribute to the device breakdown. However, the manufacturer ' s guidelines and experimental results on pulsed over-current driving of their LEDs have focused on certain operation parameters (e.g. 1KHz drive frequency with 5, 20, and 50% duty cycles). A future experiment would be to pulse one of the LEDs indefinitely and observe the effects. This can provide a baseline for the flash system ' s life expectancy and how image quality can degrade with long-term usage of the LEDs.

A custom camera flash system using LEDs was developed and inte­ grated into a machine vision platform to better capture colour and light consistent images for daytime and nighttime usage in outdoor agricul­ tural settings. LEDs had not been used as a viable active lighting source for daytime imaging due to their lack of power to overcome the sun ' s influence. This study demonstrated their operation under a pulsing mechanism which allowed them to be overcurrent-driven beyond their rated power output, thus increasing their illuminance significantly. The performance of the system was tested outdoors at an apple orchard. Analysis on the distribution of HSV channel values in the images showed an 85% decrease in standard deviation using the LED flash system compared to images taken using auto-exposure. This implied a reduction in both colour and lighting variability in images captured by the flash

system. The flash system also allowed reducing the shutter duration on the cameras which helped to lessen the effect of motion blur when the cameras captured images under high-velocity movement and ground vibrations. Motion blur from the auto-exposure setting of the camera, while moving at ~ 7 km/hr, created an average of 7 mm error when measuring objects in images. The results from this study indicated the use of LEDs as a viable light source for daytime imaging and perhaps an alternative to xenon flash lamps. Since the amount of illuminance of LEDs varies relative to the input voltage, an LED flash system can be highly customizable to fit various computer vision applications. **CRediT authorship contribution statement **
